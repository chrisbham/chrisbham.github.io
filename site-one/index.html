01 THE WHY

THE CLASSROOM IS A LEARNING ENVIRONMENT

A classroom is more than a collection of desks.

A classroom's physical environment plays an important role in shaping the experiences students have throughout the school day. Where students sit, how desks are arranged, and how easily they can move through a space can influence opportunities for interaction, participation, and engagement.

During my classroom observations, I noticed a recurring pattern: students seated toward the back of traditionally arranged classrooms often appeared more disengaged, distracted, or disconnected from instruction than those seated closer to the front.

This observation raised an important question: Could the way we organize classroom space unintentionally create barriers to student engagement and participation?

BEYOND FURNITURE ARRANGEMENT

Space influences how learning happens.

Traditional rows can provide clear sightlines and support focused individual work. Collaborative arrangements can create more opportunities for discussion and shared problem-solving. Flexible learning spaces can offer students different ways to engage with their environment.

However, no single arrangement works equally well for every activity or every student. The effectiveness of a classroom layout depends on the instructional goals, the students' needs, and how the space is used.

FOUR CONSIDERATIONS FOR INTENTIONAL DESIGN

What should classroom design support?

01

Engagement

Creating environments that encourage attention, active involvement, and meaningful participation in learning activities.

02

Relationships

Recognizing how physical proximity and opportunities for interaction can contribute to peer relationships and classroom community.

03

Flexibility

Matching classroom arrangements to instructional goals, whether students are working independently, collaborating, or participating in discussion.

04

Accessibility

Considering physical, sensory, and environmental barriers so that all students have meaningful opportunities to participate.

The goal isn't to find one perfect seating arrangement.

It is to create a classroom environment that responds thoughtfully to the people, activities, and learning taking place within it.

02 THE RESEARCH

WHAT THE EVIDENCE TELLS US

Classroom design is more than an aesthetic choice.

Research suggests that classroom seating arrangements and student proximity can influence the social and academic experiences that develop within a classroom. However, these relationships are complex. No single arrangement guarantees better engagement or achievement.

Four research perspectives help us understand how classroom environments can shape opportunities for learning and participation.

01 CLASSROOM ARRANGEMENT

The Arrangement Effect

The way desks are organized can influence how students interact with their teacher and peers, as well as how easily they can focus on different learning activities.

What the research suggests

Wannarka and Ruhl (2008) reviewed empirical research on classroom seating arrangements. Their findings suggest that rows can support on-task behaviour during independent work, while clusters and semicircles can better support interaction and discussion.

What this means for teachers

Choose arrangements based on the activity taking place, rather than assuming one layout is always best.

Wannarka & Ruhl (2008)

02 SOCIAL RELATIONSHIPS

The Proximity Effect

Physical distance can shape the opportunities students have to interact, communicate, and develop relationships with their classmates.

What the research suggests

Faur and Laursen (2022) examined classroom seating proximity and friendship formation among elementary school students. Their findings indicate that students seated near one another were more likely to develop friendship connections.

What this means for teachers

Seating decisions can influence who has regular opportunities to interact, potentially shaping the development of peer relationships.

Faur & Laursen (2022)

03 ACADEMIC ENGAGEMENT

The Peer Influence Effect

Students do not learn in isolation. The classmates around them can contribute to the social and academic environment in which learning takes place.

What the research suggests

Gremmen and colleagues (2018) investigated the importance of nearby peers for elementary students' academic engagement and achievement. Their work highlights the relevance of the immediate peer environment to students' academic experiences.

What this means for teachers

Consider peer dynamics when making seating decisions. However, placing students beside a particular classmate does not guarantee improved engagement or academic performance.

Gremmen et al. (2018); Gremmen et al. (2017)

04 INCLUSIVE DESIGN

The Inclusion Effect

Students have different needs, preferences, and ways of participating. A classroom environment should account for this variability rather than expect every student to learn comfortably in the same conditions.

What the research and frameworks suggest

CAST's Universal Design for Learning Guidelines (2024) emphasize learner variability, meaningful choice, and multiple ways to engage with learning. Minero (2017) provides practical examples of flexible classroom spaces and student seating choice.

What this means for teachers

Consider different seating options, sensory needs, movement, and opportunities for students to engage with the classroom in different ways.

CAST (2024); Minero (2017)

A BROADER PERSPECTIVE

Learning is inherently social.

Vygotsky's (1978) sociocultural perspective emphasizes the role of social interaction and guided learning in cognitive development. Although his work does not prescribe particular classroom layouts, it provides a theoretical foundation for considering how classroom environments can create opportunities for students to learn with and from one another.

Other research, including Wasendorf and colleagues (2023), has examined how seating location relates to student performance in large postsecondary lecture settings. These findings offer an additional perspective, but should not automatically be generalized to elementary or secondary classrooms.

Important distinction

Research identifies relationships between seating, proximity, peer interactions, and engagement. These relationships do not mean that furniture arrangement alone determines student success. Instructional practices, classroom culture, individual needs, and the broader learning environment all matter.
